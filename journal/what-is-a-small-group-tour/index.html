What is a small group tour, and is it worth it?

AOBy Anna Okafor, Co-founder and former trek leader
2 November 2026 · 9 min read

In short

A small group tour is a guided trip with fixed dates and a set itinerary, shared with roughly 8 to 16 other travellers and run by a professional leader. Accommodation, ground transport, activities and many meals are included. It usually costs less than a private trip with the same leader and access, and more than travelling independently, in exchange for expertise, company and no logistics.

What counts as a "small" group?

There is no official definition. In the travel industry, "small group" generally means fewer than 16 travellers, compared with 30 to 50 on a traditional coach tour. Some operators cap groups at 16, some at 14, and a few, including us, at 12. The number matters more than it sounds, because it decides almost everything else about the trip.

Twelve people fit in one minibus, around one restaurant table and into a family-run inn with six rooms. Twenty people need a coach, a hotel with a conference wing and a restaurant that takes group bookings. At 12, a farmer can invite the whole group into her kitchen; at 30, she can’t. When you compare trips, look for the maximum group size, not the average, and ask what the average actually was last year. Ours was nine.

Who goes on small group tours?

The stereotype is retirees on a coach. The reality, at least on active small-group trips, is broader. On Fernway departures in 2026:

  • About 40% of travellers came on their own, and most of them chose a group precisely so they would not be alone.
  • Around a third were couples, often people who wanted someone else to plan for once.
  • Most travellers were between 35 and 70, and a typical group spanned two or three decades.
  • Groups included travellers from an average of five countries, with English as the shared language.

What they have in common is less about age than about attitude: they are curious, comfortable with a little uncertainty, and happy to share a table with strangers who are usually friends by the third day.

What’s included, and what isn’t

Inclusions vary between operators, so always read the small print. A well-run small group tour typically includes:

Usually includedUsually not included
All accommodation, sharing a roomInternational flights
A leader for the whole tripTravel insurance
Ground transport during the tripSome lunches and dinners
Entry fees and listed activitiesDrinks, tips and optional extras
Breakfasts and many dinnersA single room (supplement)

Two things are worth checking specifically. First, whether "transfers" means airport pick-ups on any flight or only at set times. Second, whether entry fees for the headline sites, such as Machu Picchu or Petra, are included or quietly listed as optional.

What does a small group tour cost?

Prices depend heavily on the destination and comfort level, but for active small-group trips with good, characterful accommodation, a reasonable range in 2027 is $250 to $600 per person per day, excluding flights. Safaris and anywhere with expensive park fees sit at the top of that range or above it.

Here is how the same 11-day Japan itinerary compares across three ways of doing it, based on our own costings:

Way of travellingPrice per personPer day
Independent, booked yourselfAbout $3,600$330

The independent version is cheapest, but it does not include a leader, temple-lodging bookings that are hard to make from abroad, luggage forwarding or the hours of planning. The group trip shares the cost of the leader and transport between up to twelve people, which is where its value comes from.

The honest downsides

Small group travel is not for everyone, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. The trade-offs are real:

  • Fixed dates. You fit the calendar, not the other way round.
  • A shared pace. The group walks at a pace that suits most people, which may be a little slow for the fastest.
  • Less spontaneity. You can always skip an activity, but you can’t decide to stay an extra night in a village you love.
  • Other people. Most groups gel quickly. Occasionally someone doesn’t, and a good leader manages it, but you will notice.

If any of those is a deal-breaker, a tailor-made trip or a private departure will suit you better.

How to choose a good small group tour

When comparing operators, these are the questions that separate good trips from merely busy ones:

  1. What is the maximum group size? Twelve or fewer changes the experience.
  2. Who leads it? Ask whether leaders live in the region and how long they have worked with the company.
  3. How hard is it really? Look for a rating scale with hours and metres climbed, not just "moderate".
  4. Is the departure guaranteed? If not, when will you know, and what happens if it doesn’t run?
  5. Is your money protected? Payments should be held in a protected client account until you travel.
  6. What do recent travellers say? Read the three-star reviews; they tell you the most.

Questions about small group tours

Is a small group tour cheaper than travelling independently?

Usually not in pure cash terms: booking everything yourself is typically 20 to 40% cheaper. But it does not include a leader, specialist bookings or your planning time, and in places where guides and private transport are essential, a group can come out cheaper than doing it alone.

Can I join a small group tour as a couple or with friends?

Yes. Couples and friends book together all the time and share a room. If you are four or more, consider a private departure of the same itinerary, which can cost about the same per person.

Do I have to join every activity on a group tour?

No. You can skip any activity and spend the time on your own; just tell your leader. On walking days there is usually a shorter alternative or a transfer to the next stop.
